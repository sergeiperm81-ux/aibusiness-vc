date: "2026-08-04"

---

Two pieces of news crossed my desk in the same week, and together they say more than each does alone.

First: TIME, the magazine with the Person of the Year, started running advertising inside the version of its pages that AI reads. A startup called Mobian converts publisher pages into clean markdown for AI agents and inserts a sponsored block of verifiable facts about a brand. The human visitor never sees it. The agent reads it, trusts it, the facts are true, the publisher vouches for them, and carries that brand into its answers. FOX and Hearst are already in.

Second: an open-source project called OpenCLI, 27,000 stars on GitHub in a few months. It turns any website into a command line for AI agents: the agent operates the site through your own logged-in browser, clicking, filling forms, extracting data, no API needed. Their manifesto is one sentence long: a site is not a page, it is an interface.

Different companies, different ends of the market, same underlying event. **The web is splitting into two layers: one for people, one for machines.** People see design, photography, brand colors, a carefully written headline. Machines see stripped markdown, structured facts, commands. And here is the part that matters for anyone who runs a business: these two layers are already diverging, and almost nobody is managing the second one.

Think about what your company's website actually is now. For twenty years it had one audience, humans, and you polished every pixel for them. Today an increasing share of your "visitors" are agents: ChatGPT looking something up for a customer, Perplexity assembling a comparison, someone's personal assistant checking whether you deliver on Saturdays. Mobian's research puts a number on the consequence: **79% of the sources AI cites about a brand are domains the brand does not own.** Read that again. When a machine describes your business to a potential customer, four times out of five it is quoting somebody else, a review site, a Reddit thread, an outdated directory, not you.

That is what an unmanaged machine layer looks like. It is not empty; it is full of other people's versions of you.

The advertising route, paying to place your facts inside someone else's machine layer, is one response, and I understand why brands will take it. But notice what it is at the consumer's end: the agent recommends a bank, and the user has no idea that recommendation was seeded by a sponsored block the agent read somewhere. Everything in that block was true; nobody was technically deceived; and yet the person was steered without knowing it. I have spent this whole year writing that the habits of AI-mediated commerce are hardening right now, this is exactly the kind of habit I would rather we did not harden.

There is a quieter response available, and it starts at home: **put your own machine layer in order before renting space in someone else's.** Concretely, that means three unglamorous things. Make the machine version of your site exist, clean, readable pages instead of a JavaScript puzzle an agent gives up on. Make your facts findable and verifiable at the source, what you do, what it costs, what you guarantee, stated plainly on your own domain, so an agent quoting you quotes *you*. And keep it honest, the machine layer must say the same thing as the human layer, because the fastest way to get filtered out of AI answers permanently will be getting caught saying different things to different audiences.

None of this is exotic. It is the same discipline as the old web playbook, have a site, keep it truthful, make it accessible, applied to a new reader. The difference is that this reader has infinite patience, quotes you verbatim, and increasingly stands between you and every customer you will ever meet.

The split is not coming; it has happened. The only open question is whether the machine version of your business is written by you, or about you.
